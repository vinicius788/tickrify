What Is a Good Risk-Reward Ratio? The Win Rate Each One Requires

Tickrify Team · September 2026

The honest answer is that no ratio is good on its own. A risk-reward ratio is only good relative to the win rate you can actually achieve while using it, and that relationship is arithmetic rather than opinion. Here is the table, the formula behind it, what costs do to it, and the two ways traders quietly break it.

The break-even table

Risk one unit, target R units. The win rate at which you end up flat, before costs:

Risk-rewardBreak-even win rateWhat it means in practice
1:0.566.7%Two out of three must win just to stand still. Very hard to hold for long.
1:150.0%A coin flip with costs on top, which means a coin flip that loses.
1:1.540.0%The most common realistic zone for intraday work.
1:233.3%Two losers out of three is still flat. This is the number most traders misjudge.
1:325.0%Three losers out of four is flat, and losing streaks get long.
1:420.0%Swing territory. Requires patience the account rarely gets.
1:516.7%Rarely reached in full, because the exit usually happens first.

Break-even is not a target, it is a floor. Clearing it by a point or two is statistically indistinguishable from not clearing it over any sample you will personally trade.

The formula, and why it is that

Expectancy per trade, in units of risk:

E = (W × R) − (1 − W)

where W is the win rate as a decimal and R is the reward-to-risk multiple. Set E to zero and solve:

Break-even W = 1 ÷ (1 + R)

That is the whole table. At R = 2, W = 1/3. At R = 3, W = 1/4. It is worth committing to memory, because it is the fastest way to evaluate any claim anyone makes about a strategy. A "90 percent win rate" system running at 1:0.2 needs 83.3 percent just to break even, which reframes the headline considerably.

A win rate without its risk-reward ratio is not a statistic. It is a decoration.

What costs do to the threshold

Spread, commission, slippage and financing are all paid regardless of outcome. Express the round-trip cost as c, a fraction of the money you were risking, and the threshold becomes:

Break-even W = (1 + c) ÷ (1 + R)

With costs worth 10 percent of your risk per trade, which is ordinary for retail on a modest stop:

Risk-rewardBefore costsWith c = 0.10With c = 0.25
1:150.0%55.0%62.5%
1:1.540.0%44.0%50.0%
1:233.3%36.7%41.7%
1:325.0%27.5%31.3%
1:420.0%22.0%25.0%

The important structural point: c is a function of your stop distance, not of your account size. A 2 pip round-trip cost is 4 percent of a 50 pip stop and 40 percent of a 5 pip stop. This is why scalping needs a far higher hit rate than the textbook numbers suggest, and why the same strategy can be profitable on a four hour chart and unprofitable on a one minute chart with nothing else changed.

Why a higher ratio is not free

The obvious move after seeing the table is to widen every target. It does not work, for a reason worth stating precisely: a target further away is hit less often. Push from 1:2 to 1:4 and the break-even threshold falls from 33.3 to 20 percent, but your actual hit rate falls too, often by more than the threshold did. Whether the trade is an improvement depends on which fell faster, and the only way to know is your own recorded data.

Three second-order effects that make high ratios harder to hold than the arithmetic suggests:

  • Streak length. At a 25 percent win rate, runs of six, eight or ten losses are routine rather than exceptional. The system can be perfectly sound and still feel broken for weeks, which is how correct strategies get abandoned.
  • Time in trade. A distant target means longer exposure, which means more scheduled events, more overnight risk and more financing cost.
  • Discipline drift. The longer a trade runs, the more chances you get to interfere with it. Which leads to the next section.

Planned ratio versus realised ratio

This is where most plans die. You plan 1:3. Price runs to 1:1, you move the stop to break-even, price retraces, you are out flat. Next trade, same thing, but you take partial profit at 1:1 and the rest stops out at break-even. Your losers, meanwhile, are still full losers, because nothing interferes with a trade going against you.

Your journal says 1:3. Your account is trading something closer to 1:1 with the upside amputated. At 1:1 you need 50 percent, not 25, and you have been benchmarking yourself against the wrong line the whole time.

The fix is not "never move a stop". It is to measure the realised ratio: total profit on winners divided by total loss on losers, over your last fifty trades. Compare that number to the one in your plan. If it is materially lower, either stop interfering or lower the planned ratio to match what you actually do, and check the threshold for that ratio instead.

How to choose yours

  1. Start from the chart, not the rule. The stop goes where the idea is wrong, and the target goes at the next structural level price must reach. The ratio is the output of those two decisions, not an input you impose.
  2. Reject the trade if the ratio is not there. If the honest stop and the honest target produce 1:0.8, the answer is no trade, not a tighter stop. Moving the stop closer to make the ratio work is how a defined-risk trade becomes a noise-stopped one.
  3. Compare against your real win rate. Fifty trades minimum, and the cost-adjusted threshold from the table above.
  4. Leave margin. Aim for the break-even threshold plus a clear buffer, because your sample is small and your future self is worse than your backtest.

This is also the single most useful thing to demand from any analysis, human or machine: a stop level that is justified by structure and a target that is reachable, so the ratio is a consequence of the read rather than a number attached afterwards. FAQ

What is a good risk-reward ratio?

There is no universal answer. A ratio is good when your real, cost-adjusted win rate clears the break-even line for it with margin. That line is 1 divided by (1 plus the ratio).

What win rate do I need for 1:2?

33.3 percent before costs, around 36.7 percent with costs worth 10 percent of your risk. Aim several points above that to be meaningfully profitable rather than flat.

Is 1:3 realistic?

Only with the win rate that comes with it, typically 25 to 40 percent, and the long losing streaks that go with it. Raising the ratio does not add edge, it reshapes the same edge.

How do costs change the threshold?

The threshold becomes (1 plus c) divided by (1 plus R), where c is the round-trip cost as a fraction of risk. Costs hurt most at tight stops.

Why is my realised ratio lower than my planned one?

Early exits. Break-even stops and partial profits cap the winners that pay for the losers, while the losers stay full size. Measure total winner profit divided by total loser loss and use that ratio, not the planned one.
